Radiation  In the year of the accident and the following year, there was an increase in the number of resin ducts (Musaev, 1996; Skuterud et al., 1994). The normal structure of earlywood (EW) tracheids is disturbed by interruptions in the rows, insertion of new tracheid rows (Musaev, 1996), and ‘forking’ (bifurcation) of tracheid rows (Kozubov and Taskaev, 1994; Schmitt et al., 2000; Tulik and Kozakiewicz, 2005). Some tracheids were 2–3 times larger than normal EW tracheids and had irregular shapes, such as oval, round, or nearly isodiametric forms (Tulik, 2001). Latewood (LW) tracheids maintained typical wall thickness (Musaev, 1996). Affected LW tracheids have larger lumina, thinner walls, and distorted shape (Clark and Hamilton, 1968). Rays became curved and wider, traumatic resin ducts deformed, and groups of parenchyma cells were observed (Kozubov and Taskaev, 1994; Tulik and Kozakiewicz, 2005). Thin-walled cells of a parenchymatous nature, stained brownish-red, appeared randomly in both EW and LW, indicating high lignin content (Tulik, 2001). The 1987 tree ring shows similar but less pronounced changes (Schmitt et al., 2000), with increased parenchymatisation and wider rays (Musaev, 1996), followed by a growth reduction and missing rings  (Woodwell and Miller, 1963) in following years (1988-1989; (Musaev, 1995)). 

The ends of the tracheids are irregular (Tulik and Kozakiewicz, 2005), distorted, sometimes split, elongated, or flattened. The tracheids have funnel-shaped pits on their walls, but the pitting pattern is irregular. The typical single -row arrangement of pits, two -row or entirely irregular pits (Tulik and Kozakiewicz, 2005). In the walls of macerated tracheids, structures were observed neither funnel -shaped pits nor window -

like pits (Tulik, 2001). 

Tracheids length is not affected (Clark and Hamilton, 1968) or increased tracheids length (Tulik, 2001). Trachareids wall structure is changed in both S2 and S3 layers (Tulik and Rusin, 2005). Untypical clusters of rays connected with their irregular shape and “stretching” parenchyma cells (Tulik and Kozakiewicz, 2005). 

Frost ring  Tracheids are thin-walled (Lee et al., 2007; Schweingruber, 2007), sometimes extremely thin-walled, and greatly collapsed occurring alongside thicker-walled uncollapsed or less collapsed tracheids. The thin walls ranged from consisting solely of compound middle lamella, externally coated with irregular deposits of dense material, to having modified secondary wall materials differing in their density and texture (Glerum and Farrar, 1966; Lee et al., 2007). Tracheids are often highly collapsed, tangentially flattened, axial tracheids deviate from the radial course of alignment (Glerum and Farrar, 1966; Lee et al., 2007; Schweingruber, 2007). Callus tissue and morphologically regenerating tracheids are present (Schweingruber, 2007). Rays are highly distended (Rhoads, 1923), displaced (Fritts, 1976; Glock, 1951), often undergoing a knee-shaped bending (Rhoads, 1923; Schweingruber, 2007). 

Tracheids are much shorter and have blunt end walls (Glerum and Farrar, 1966). Rays often consist of an increased number of parenchyma cells 14). 

The ray parenchyma cannot be distinguished from the traumatic parenchyma. The tracheids are irregular in shape and abnormally short (Glerum and Farrar, 1966). 

IADF  A dark band of latewood-like cells within the earlywood, or a light band of earlywood-like cells within the latewood, is characterized by a gradual or abrupt transition to neighboring cells (De Micco et al., 2016; Kuo and Mc Ginnes, 1973). In earlywood (EW), the double cell wall thickness is equal to or greater than the cell lumen diameter (De Micco et al., 2016), with a more or less fluent transition to flattened tracheids with thin cell walls (Schweingruber, 2007). 

Pits are smaller and more elliptical, comparable to surrounding transition wood (Mayer et al., 2020).
