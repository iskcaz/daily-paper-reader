# Trifluoroacetic Acid (TFA) Exceeds All Other Extractable Organofluorine in Drinking Water from Eastern Massachusetts, United States 

# Jahred M. Liddie, * Euna Kim, Faiz Haque, Anton Roche, Ethan J. Sontarp, and Elsie M. Sunderland *

Cite This: https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.estlett.6c00404 Read Online 

# ACCESS Metrics & More Article Recommendations *sı Supporting Information 

ABSTRACT: Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are present in drinking water consumed by ∼200 million United States (US) residents, but many are not detected by routine monitoring methods. Here, we aimed to better characterize the organofluorine mass budget in US drinking water samples from 19 sites in eastern Massachusetts. Extractable organofluorine (EOF) was detected at 16/17 sites served by community water systems (CWS) using conventional treatment techniques (coagulation, flocculation, clarification, and filtration). At these sites, a suite of 37 targeted PFAS accounted for 6 −68% of the EOF and suspect screening identified fluorinated agrochemicals in almost all samples. High EOF concentrations in untreated source waters for two CWS that used granular activated carbon (GAC) and ion-exchange (IX) treatment, were reduced below detection in finished drinking water samples. Our EOF measurements were not selective for trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), so we measured concentrations in triplicate samples at one conventional treatment site and from the IX and GAC sites. At these sites, TFA concentrations were higher than those of EOF and were unaffected by the treatment type. Results of this pilot study show GAC and IX treatment removed virtually all organofluorine in drinking water other than TFA. 

KEYWORDS: drinking water, water treatment, PFAS, contamination, ultrashort-chain PFAS 

# ■ INTRODUCTION 

Past estimates suggest approximately 200 million people in the United States (US) consume drinking water with detectable concentrations of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS). 1

However, standard methods, such as 1633 by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) 2 that includes 40 PFAS, do not include all of the PFAS present in drinking water samples. Many more are thought to be used in modern commerce but lack analytical standards needed for routine detection. 3,4 Comparing bulk extractable organofluorine (EOF) concentrations and targeted PFAS measurements is useful for estimating the magnitude of missing PFAS and is often referred to as unidentified EOF. 5−7 However, some EOF measurements are not selective for some ultrashort PFAS such as trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), meaning their relative con-tribution to the organofluorine mass budget is uncertain. Recent studies have highlighted growing concerns associated with rapidly increasing TFA concentrations in the global environment due to human activity. 8−10 TFA is produced directly for various industrial and commercial products by the fluorochemical industry. 10,11 It is also indirectly produced in the atmosphere as a degradation product of fluorinated gases and in aqueous systems from degradation of some PFAS precursors, fluorinated pharmaceuticals, and fluorinated agro-chemicals. 8,10,12 TFA concentrations often exceed perfluor-oalkyl acids (PFAA) with seven or more perfluorinated carbons ( npfc ≥ 7) in aqueous samples. 10,13 −15 However, a comparison of TFA and EOF concentrations in US drinking water samples is not available and is a goal of this work. The main objective of this work was to conduct a pilot analysis of the magnitude, composition, and variability in organofluorine in US drinking water among community water systems (CWS) using different treatment technologies. To do this, we measured EOF as a bulk proxy for most PFAS in 19 CWS in eastern Massachusetts (MA), US. We compared EOF concentrations to a suite of 37 targeted PFAS and calculated unidentified EOF as the difference between the two measure-ments. We also identified suspect PFAS in the same extracts. # ■ MATERIALS AND METHODS 

Sample Collection 

Between May and August 2024, we collected finished tap water samples from faucets in publicly accessible locations (e.g., libraries, town halls, and parks) served by 19 different CWS in eastern MA, USA. Additional triplicate samples were obtained between May and July 2025 from site 4, which had the highest unidentified EOF concentration in treated drinking water, and two sites that had installed treatment capacity for removing PFAS using granular activated carbon (GAC) and ion exchange (IX). We collected untreated source water and finished drinking water from the two sites that had installed GAC and IX treatment processes to remove PFAS. Samples were collected in 1-L high-density polyethylene bottles precleaned with methanol and Milli-Q water. At every other site, we collected a sample duplicate ( n = 9) or field blank ( n = 10). Samples were stored at −20 °C prior to analysis and analyzed within 1 month of collection. Additional details on sample collection are provided in the Supporting Information (SI) section 1.1. In our study design, we selected 16 sites from those that had previously reported detectable targeted PFAS in government monitoring from 2024 to ensure detectable concentrations of EOF would be present in most samples. 16 We selected one site that did not have detectable PFAS in drinking water (<2 ng/L) based on previously compiled statewide data from 2019 to 2023 (site 17). 17 

Metadata on treatment processes were obtained from online searches, consumer confidence reports, and treatment process data from the US EPA. 18 CWS supplying drinking water to 17/19 sites used conventional treatment processes (including coagulation, flocculation, clarification, and/or filtration) (sites 1 −17) and the other two CWS additionally used GAC and IX treatment, respectively. CWS supplying drinking water samples in this study varied in size (37% medium, serving 3.3 −10K people; 63% large or very large, serving >10K) and sourced both groundwater (58%) and surface water (42%). Most water systems (63%) fluoridated their treated drinking water. 19 

EOF Analysis 

Details of the materials used for sample analysis and solid-phase extraction (SPE) techniques, following established methods, 2,20,21 are provided in the SI sections 1.2 −1.3. We quantified EOF concentrations using a combustion unit from Analytik Jena (Jena, Germany), 920 Absorber Module, and 930 Compact IC Flex ion chromatograph from Metrohm (Herisau, Switzerland) at Harvard University. 20,21 The average relative percent difference between sample duplicate injections was 6%. Methanol blanks were run before and after the calibration points and after every 12 samples to account for any possible contamination from the solvents used in the analysis. Batch-specific method detection limits (MDLs) were determined based on the dilution factor multiplied by the mean plus three times the standard deviation of the extraction blanks and ranged from 8.8 to 27 ng F/L. For consistency, EOF concentrations are reported in this study based on detection above the maximum MDL (27 ng F/L). The SI contains additional details on sample analysis and quality assurance/quality control (QA/QC) procedures for the EOF analysis (SI section 1.4). 

Targeted PFAS Analysis of EOF Extracts 

We analyzed concentrations of 37 targeted PFAS (C3 −C12) in EOF extracts (Tables S1 and S2 contain additional details on the targeted analytes). Extracts were spiked with internal standards prior to quantification (see SI sections 1.3 −1.5 for details). Detection and quantification were performed using a Vanquish Flex ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatograph (ThermoFisher) coupled with a quadruple Orbitrap Exploris 120 high-resolution mass spectrometer instrument (UHPLC-HRMS, ThermoFisher) in negative electrospray ionization mode (ESI −) at Harvard University, following prior methods. 21,22 The instrumental method used a Thermo Acclaim RSLC 120 C18 separation column (2.2 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm).

Trifluoracetic Acid (TFA) Analysis 

The high polarity of ultrashort-chain PFAS means that extraction recoveries using standard SPE methods are often low. 14,23,24 Further, we estimated that only ∼50% of any TFA present in extracts would be captured in EOF measurements by the CIC at the concentrations analyzed in this study (see SI section 1.6 and Table S6 for details). We therefore used a different extraction and analytical method optimized for high recovery to measure TFA concentrations at the same sites previously sampled. Using triplicate drinking water samples from three sites (site 4 and the systems with GAC filtration and IX), we used a liquid −liquid extraction (LLE) 24 method that leverages pH-programmed partition-ing and evaporative transfer of MTBE-retained PFAS to the aqueous residue. The LLE method 24 used an Atlantis Premier BEH C18 AX column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) to separate analytes in the same UHPLC system as for other targeted PFAS. For TFA, the average relative percent difference was 2%, the recovery was 83%, and the MDL was 11.5 ng/L. Additional details on this method and quality assurance data are provided in SI section 1.6. 

Suspect Screening Analysis 

Suspect PFAS and other fluorinated compounds were identified based on previously compiled lists of fluorinated agrochemicals, pharma-ceuticals, and known human metabolites. 25 −28 Confidence levels of 2a-2b were assigned following the scales defined in Charbonnet et al. (2014) and Schymanski et al. (2022). 29,30 Additional details on the methods used are provided in SI sections 1.7 and 1.8 and QA/QC details are shown in Table S7. 

# ■ RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

Substantial Unidentified EOF in CWS with Conventional Drinking Water Treatment 

At 16/17 sites served by CWS with conventional treatment (coagulation, flocculation, clarification, and/or filtration), we detected a high proportion of EOF not accounted for by targeted PFAS that ranged from 32 to 95% (Figure 1, sites 1 −

16; Table S8 shows all EOF results). EOF (1.6 to 14.0 nM F) was detected at all 16 sites that previously reported detectable targeted PFAS in state monitoring programs. 16 Concentrations of EOF were in the same range as a 2016 pilot study in MA (0.84 −8.2 nM F, n = 5 systems), 31 a 2022 pilot study in cities near Taihu Lake, China (6.6 −21 nM F, n = 4 systems), 6 and a 2023 tap water study in Shanghai, China (10 −37 nM F, n = 5 systems). 15 By contrast, EOF concentrations were below detection (MDL = 1.4 nM F) at the site that previously reported targeted PFAS below detection (Figure 1, site 17). Despite this pattern, we found a weak and insignificant association between targeted PFAS and unidentified EOF concentrations across the 17 sites with conventional treatment (Kendall’s rank correlation: 0.12, p = 0.53; SI section 1.8). Additional larger scale studies are needed to confirm whether routinely measured PFAS are correlated with unidentified EOF. Several Fluorinated Agrochemicals Identified as Suspects in Drinking Water 

Suspect screening analysis revealed six fluorinated agro-chemicals present in drinking water samples at confidence levels of 2a-2b (Figure 2 and S1 and Table S11). 29,30 These included three herbicides (fluazifop-P, flurprimidol, and sulfentrazone), two fungicides (flutriafol and flutolanil), and a metabolite of an insecticide (fipronil sulfone). Fluazifop-P is a degradation product of fluazifop-P butyl, which is used globally to control grasses. 33 High peak areas for fluazifop-P butyl (range: 1.5 × 10 4−2.3 × 10 7) were detected at all sites with conventional drinking water treatment. In prior work, widespread detections of fluazifop-P butyl were reported in a kettle lake and river waters from an adjacent region. 21 

Other suspect compounds were identified at lower prevalences (6 −12%; Figure 2). We found moderate correlations between the number of identified suspects and unidentified EOF concentrations and the total area of identified suspects and unidentified EOF among the 17 sites with conventional treatment processes (Kendall’s correlation coefficients: 0.32 with p = 0.15 and 0.44 with p = 0.03, respectively). Several of the agrochemicals identified as suspects met the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development definition of PFAS. 34 PFAS can be incorporated into agrochemical products intentionally or due to leaching from fluorinated agrochemical storage containers. 28 Prior work in the states of North Carolina, 35 California, 36 and Iowa 37 has also identified fluorinated agrochemicals in drinking water or applied in proximity to drinking water sources. Additional research on the occurrence of fluorinated agrochemicals in US drinking water supplies, degradation of agrochemicals to ultrashort-chain PFAS, 28 and potential human and ecological health impacts is therefore warranted. 38 

GAC and IX Treatment Reduced EOF to below Detectable Levels 

We examined the organofluorine composition of raw and treated drinking water samples at two additional CWS that used GAC and IX treatment and compared organofluorine concentrations and composition to the conventional treatment sites (Figure 3). EOF concentrations in the raw drinking water ranged between 6.3 nM F (GAC site) and 22.3 nM F (IX site) compared to peak concentrations of 14.0 nM F at site 4 of the conventional treatment locations (Figure 1). Following treatment, EOF concentrations at both the GAC and IX sites were reduced to below detectable values (Figure 3A). The sum of 37 targeted PFAS was reduced by 48% in the finished drinking water from the GAC site relative to the raw water and by >99% (below all MDLs) at the IX site (Figure 3B). At the GAC site, reductions in targeted PFAS in finished drinking water compared to raw water varied by chain length and functional group (Table S12). TFA Concentrations Unaffected by Treatment and Persistently High 

TFA was detected in 100% of drinking water samples analyzed (n = 6 untreated samples, n = 9 treated samples; Table S13 shows all results). TFA concentrations measured in triplicate samples from site 4, supplied by a CWS using conventional treatment (Figure 3C), were approximately four times higher than EOF (Figure 3A) and ranged from 1020 to 1090 ng/L (53.7 −57.2 nM F equivalent). At the CWS using GAC and IX treatment, TFA concentrations were similar between raw and finished drinking water samples (Figure 3C). Average TFA concentrations were 2.5% higher in finished drinking water compared to raw water at the GAC site and were reduced by 7% at the IX site. In raw samples, TFA was approximately 7-fold higher than all other EOF at the GAC site and approximately 3-fold higher than EOF at the IX site (Figure 3A,C). These results highlight that TFA dominates the organofluorine mass budget of these drinking water samples and was relatively unaffected by treatment type. The SPE method used to extract EOF can sometimes recover a small fraction of the TFA present in samples. 24 

However, EOF was removed to below detectable levels at the GAC and IX sites following treatment, contrasting no effect on directly measured TFA concentrations. This suggests TFA was a minor fraction of the unidentified EOF shown in Figure 3. TFA concentrations measured in this study fell within the same ranges of drinking water samples measured in past studies (33 −3800 ng/L) based in Indiana (US), Sweden, The Netherlands, Germany, and France (Table S14). 13,14,39 −41 

TFA concentrations observed in this work were below preliminary human health guidelines established in Germany in 2020 (guideline: 60,000 ng/L) 42 and The Netherlands in 2023 (2200 ng/L). 43 However, several studies have noted large increases in groundwater concentrations over time. 44,45 

Further, TFA has been detected globally across other environmental matrixes 46,47 and human urine and serum. 46 −48 Findings from this work thus further emphasize concerns related to increasing global environmental concen-trations and associated human exposures. 

Study Implications 

In drinking water samples supplied by CWS from eastern MA with conventional treatment, we detected large quantities of unidentified organofluorine ranging from 32 to 95% of the detectable EOF. Similar proportions of unidentified EOF were measured in raw water from two CWS with GAC and IX treatment, which are recommended for PFAS removal by the US EPA. 49,50 In finished drinking water samples from the GAC and IX sites, EOF was removed to below detectable levels, emphasizing the effectiveness of these treatment technologies for removing both regulated PFAS and some unidentified PFAS. Our results for CWS using processes effective for PFAS removal are comparable to those reported in both residential point of use and treatment plant studies on GAC and IX, 51 −54 

but contrast low removal efficiencies for EOF (<25%) by US wastewater treatment facilities with tertiary treatment. 25 

By contrast, treatment had little effect on TFA concen-trations in finished drinking water. TFA concentrations were much higher than all other organofluorine detected in all drinking water samples analyzed. These results pose a concern given rapid rises in environmental concentrations noted in recent literature. 9,44,45 Suspect screening also identified several fluorinated agrochemicals in drinking water samples such as fluazifop-P, which has also been detected in humans, highlighting drinking water as a potential exposure source. 55 

These pilot data emphasize the need for additional monitoring and risk evaluation for fluorinated agrochemicals and ultrashort PFAS exposures from drinking water.
