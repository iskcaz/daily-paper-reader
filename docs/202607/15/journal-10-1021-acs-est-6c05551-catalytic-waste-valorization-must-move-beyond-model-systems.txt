# Catalytic Waste Valorization Must Move beyond Model Systems 

# Shuai Yue, Guanshu Zhao, * Pengfei Wang, * and Sihui Zhan *

Cite This: Environ. Sci. Technol. 2026, 60, 18379−18382 Read Online 

# ACCESS Metrics & More Article Recommendations 

KEYWORDS: waste recycling, resource security, industrial resilience, carbon mitigation 

# Amid growing resource scarcity and environmental pressure, waste recycling is becoming a core pillar of green development. 1 Policy frameworks in China, the Euro-pean Union, and the United States have all emphasized recyclable-resource utilization, secondary raw-material markets and improved recycling systems. 2−4 Together, these efforts reflect a broader shift: waste is increasingly viewed not as a disposal burden, but as a strategic resource base linked to resource security, industrial resilience, and carbon mitigation. Yet current recycling systems remain dominated by physical and metallurgical routes. Plastics are still mainly recycled through sorting, washing, shredding, and melt reprocessing, while spent batteries and electronic waste generally require pretreatment, separation, and pyro- or hydrometallurgical recovery of valuable metals. 5−7 Although indispensable, these routes often involve high-temperature processing, intensive reagent use, and repeated separation steps, which reduce economic margins and value retention. The key challenge is therefore not simply to recover matter, but to recover it in forms suitable for high-value reintegration into manufacturing. This is why emerging catalytic technologies deserve greater attention. Compared with conventional recycling routes, catalytic processes can reduce energy demand, chemical consumption, and secondary pollution while improving product selectivity (Figure 1). By controlling bond activation, interfacial electron transfer, and local reaction environments, catalytic systems offer opportunities to convert heterogeneous wastes into targeted chemicals, fuels, and reusable precursors with greater precision. In plastics recycling, catalytic routes are expanding chemical recycling beyond mechanical reprocessing and nonselective cracking. Instead, thermocatalysis, photocatalysis, photothermal catalysis, and electrocatalysis should play complementary roles according to polymer type, contamination level, target product, and energy context. Catalytic innovation is also reshaping battery recycling by shifting the field from bulk metallurgical extraction toward selective recovery and functional regeneration. Emerging electrochemical and interfacial strategies have enabled near-complete extraction of Li, Ni, Mn, and Co from spent cathodes, as well as high-efficiency, high-purity lithium recovery. 13,14 These approaches may reduce acid consumption and energy intensity while preserving more of the structural and compositional value embedded in cathode materials. A similar transition is emerging in electronic waste. Conventional routes still rely on dismantling, crushing, sorting, and metallurgical extraction, but catalytic methods are opening new possibilities for selective recovery under milder con-ditions. Su et al. 15 used contact-electro-catalysis as a metal-free and recyclable strategy for selective gold extraction from e-waste leachates. This direction is particularly significant because e-waste contains precious, base, and critical metals in highly complex matrices, and bulk extraction often sacrifices selectivity while generating additional residues. Catalytic strategies could therefore help shift e-waste recycling from mass extraction toward precision separation of scarce elements. Taken together, plastics, batteries, and e-waste reveal a unifying theme: the transformative value of catalysis lies not only in accelerating reactions, but in controlling where atoms go, which bonds are broken or preserved, and whether waste is converted into low-grade secondary material or high-value functional products. That conceptual shift distinguishes next-generation waste valorization from conventional end-of-pipe treatment. However, catalytic waste valorization remains far from practical deployment. The key challenge is not whether catalysts can activate bonds or recover elements in model systems, but whether they can do so reproducibly, selectively, and economically in heterogeneous waste streams. For example, chlorine-containing plastics, additives, pigments, and food residues in postconsumer plastics can alter product distributions or poison active sites; graphite, Al/Cu current collectors, binders, and electrolyte residues in industrial black mass can hinder selective metal recovery; and base metals, halides, and organic residues in e-waste leachates can compete with precious-metal recovery. 16 −18 This gap explains why many elegant laboratory demonstrations have not matured into pilot-scale or commercially relevant processes. To close this gap, three shifts are urgently needed. First, catalytic technologies must be designed around real waste streams rather than idealized model systems. This requires tolerance to mixed polymers, additives, pigments, aged cathodes, variable battery chemistries, industrial black mass, competing metals, halides, organics, and fluctuating solids loading. Stability under variable moisture, pretreatment history, and impurity accumulation will also be essential. Second, catalytic waste recovery must be judged not only by conversion, selectivity, or product value, but also by measurable environmental and economic benefits at the system level. LCA and TEA should therefore be used early to determine whether catalytic routes reduce energy use, emissions, reagent demand, and secondary pollution relative to incumbent processes, and whether catalyst cost, lifetime, regeneration, feedstock variability, and downstream separation can support practical deployment. Third, governments and industry should accelerate trans-lation through pilot-scale demonstrations, standardized testing protocols, and shared infrastructure for waste characterization, process benchmarking, and product certification. Progress will require closer coordination among catalysis researchers, process engineers, recyclers, manufacturers, and regulators, because no catalytic platform can be deployed independently of feedstock logistics, separation technology, or market specifications. Catalytic waste valorization should also be embedded into circular economy governance frameworks that reward high-value, low-impact resource recovery. More broadly, catalytic recycling should be designed as an integrated process rather than an isolated reaction step. Pretreatment, reaction engineering, separation, and product use should be co-optimized from the outset. In many cases, the most attractive catalyst may not be the one with the highest intrinsic activity, but the one that tolerates impure feeds, simplifies separation, or produces outputs that can directly re-enter supply chains. Ultimately, the future of catalytic waste recycling will depend less on record conversion or selectivity in model systems than on performance under realistic environmental and economic constraints. The next phase of the field should therefore be judged by catalyst durability, continuous-process validation, system integration, and evidence that recovered products can re-enter manufacturing chains at meaningful scale.
