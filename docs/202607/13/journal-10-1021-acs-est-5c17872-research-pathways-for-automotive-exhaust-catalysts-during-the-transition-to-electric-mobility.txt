ABSTRACT: The global automotive market has long been dominated by internal combustion vehicles (ICVs), which have provided essential mobility but also emitted air pollutants that harm human health and environment. Exhaust treatment catalysts have played a central role in mitigating these emissions, allowing ICVs to remain viable for decades. However, despite advances in catalyst technology, the continued reliance on fossil-fuel vehicles has driven steady increases in atmospheric CO 2, contributing to climate change and intensifying global environmental risks. In recent years, the rapid rise of electric vehicles (EVs), which eliminate tailpipe emissions, has prompted renewed discussion about the future relevance of automotive catalysts. This transition raises key questions: How quickly can EVs replace ICVs? What environmental challenges will persist during this shift? What role will catalytic technologies play in the evolving transportation landscape? In this contribution, we examine the environmental motivations behind EV adoption and assess their benefits and limitations using recent market and emissions data. We highlight constraints, including adoption barriers, hybrid vehicle cold-start emissions, and the difficulty of electrifying heavy-duty transport, that ensure catalysts will remain essential. Finally, we outline pathways for catalyst researchers to redefine their focus and remain impactful in the coming decades. 

KEYWORDS: Electric vehicles, Internal combustion engines, Hybrid vehicle emissions, Cold-start pollution, Automotive catalysts, Heavy-duty transport decarbonization, Environmental policy analysis 

1. INTRODUCTION 

The rapid pace of technological advancement often leads to the replacement of even the most widely used and seemingly indispensable technologies. Entire markets can shift as new innovations emerge. A prime example is the evolution of photography: film cameras once dominated the industry, only to be overtaken by digital cameras, which themselves have now largely been supplanted by smartphone cameras. However, not all companies that specialized in film cameras disappeared. Some, like Fujifilm and Kodak, successfully adapted by leveraging their expertise to expand into diverse fields such as medical imaging, diagnostic equipment, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals. This demonstrates that while the emergence of new technologies inevitably diminishes the relevance of older ones, it also presents opportunities for researchers and companies to pivot and innovate. Therefore, those engaged in developing existing technologies must remain highly attuned to market shifts, as the adaptation can be the key to continued success. The automotive market is experiencing rapid changes, particularly as electric vehicles (EVs) gain traction. This shift demands new technologies, posing a significant challenge for researchers in automotive catalysis, a field long devoted to mitigating pollutants in exhaust gases of internal combustion vehicles (ICVs). 1−3 Automotive catalysts were originally developed to address environmental problems such as fine dust emissions or nitrogen oxides (NO x) emissions, and they are widely regarded as one of the most important successes in catalyst research. However, EVs do not require exhaust catalysts as they do not use fuels. Does this mean that automotive catalysis will soon become obsolete? Although it may be difficult for automotive catalysts to maintain the same level of influence as before, it is unlikely that the field will disappear entirely. While EVs are growing rapidly in market share, they still face hurdles, including a so-called “chasm” in adoption, a concept from technology diffusion theory that refers to the critical gap between early adopters and the mainstream market, where many promising innovations struggle to gain widespread acceptance. 2. SCOPE AND DATA SOURCES 

The goal of this study is to evaluate the environmental impacts of vehicle electrification and to identify persistent emission challenges relevant to catalytic technologies. To this end, a broad range of statistical data was collected and interpreted in the context of this objective. This study was limited to literature and data sets directly relevant to transportation electrification, hybrid vehicle emissions, cold-start behavior, and catalytic emission-control technologies. Primary data sources were restricted to data sets reported by international organizations and governmental agencies, including the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), International Energy Agency (IEA), U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), and U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). In addition, reports from recognized think tanks and policy research organizations, such as Ember and the International Council on Clean Trans-portation (ICCT), were incorporated. Where appropriate, analyses from consulting and industry sources, including Kearney, Ernst and Young, and Boston Consulting Group, as well as peer-reviewed research articles, were used to comple-ment data interpretation, provided that these analyses were based on authoritative data sets. Particular emphasis was placed on the studies providing the quantitative analyses of vehicle electrification and catalytic emission control. 

3. THE ENVIRONMENTAL CRISIS AND THE TRANSITION TO CLEANER TRANSPORTATION 

Although EVs have a long development history, their recent surge in attention has been relatively sudden. The primary driving force behind the recent shift from ICVs to EVs is their environmental advantage, namely, the absence of direct CO 2

and pollutant emissions. This section will explore the reasons why EVs have emerged as one of the most important solutions to today’s energy and environmental challenges. 

3.1. The Growing Threat of Fossil Fuels, Air Pollution, and Climate Change 

Air pollution and global warming are causing severe environ-mental crises, increasingly threatening human lives. The melting of ice at the North and South Poles is raising sea levels, while rising global temperatures are intensifying climate disasters. In 2023, the WMO issued a red alert, reporting that the global average temperature had risen approximately 1.45 

°C above preindustrial levels, making it the hottest year on record. 4 The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report warns that if greenhouse gas emissions continue at current levels, global temperatures could rise by 3 °C or more by 2100, increasing both the frequency and intensity of climate disasters. 5 Beyond global warming, air pollutants such as carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen oxides (NO x) and fine particulate matter (PM) pose serious threats to human health. For example, between 1980 and 2020, PM air pollution caused an estimated 135 million premature deaths worldwide. 6 Addressing these environmental crises is imperative to ensuring a sustainable future for man-kind. Air pollution and global warming are driven in large part by humanity’s heavy reliance on fossil fuels as the foundation of modern economic and industrial systems. As of 2024, fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and natural gas still accounted for ∼60% of global energy consumption. 7 Beyond energy production, they also serve as feedstocks for a wide range of chemical products, including plastics and synthetic fibers such as polyester. For example, global propylene production reached about 121.3 million metric tons in 2023, while ethylene remained the highest-volume petrochemical globally. 8 In addition, most plastics are derived from fossil fuel feedstocks, primarily natural gas and crude oil byproducts. 9 The combustion of fossil fuels emits CO 2, a major greenhouse gas, along with pollutants such as CO, NO x, and sulfur oxides (SO x) (Figure 1), underscoring the importance of reducing reliance on fossil fuel-based systems and developing cleaner alternatives.

3.2. Technological Solutions for Emission Reduction 

Recognizing the severe impacts of air pollutants, researchers and policymakers have developed various solutions to mitigate emissions. Among these, catalyst technology has proven particularly effective, enabling the conversion of harmful pollutants into less toxic substances. For example, CO can be oxidized into CO 2, while NO x can be reduced to N 2

through catalytic reactions. The key advantage of catalyst technology lies in its ability to selectively promote chemical reactions at lower temperatures, making pollution control more efficient. A prime example of its success is the improvement in air quality across Los Angeles, a city once infamous for severe photochemical smog caused by vehicle emissions. In the past, NO x and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from millions of vehicles reacted under sunlight to form ground-level ozone (O 3), the principal component of smog. However, the introduction of catalytic converters, which transform CO, NO x, and VOCs into less harmful products, has led to substantial declines in vehicle emissions and sustained reductions in ambient pollutants. 10 The improvement in air quality across the U.S. over the past few decades also highlights the success of human efforts to mitigate pollution through technological advancements; between 1980 and 2023, concentrations of key air pollutants have decreased substan-tially: CO by 88% (Figure S1a), SO 2 by 95% (Figure S1b), NO 2 by 61% (Figure S1c), and PM2.5 by 36% (Figure S1d). 11 

Hence, while air pollution remains a major global challenge, advancements in catalyst technology demonstrate humanity’s ability to tackle environmental crises through technological innovation. Just as air quality has improved with catalytic solutions, continued technological development can help address other environmental challenges. Despite these advancements, however, efforts to reduce CO 2

emissions have been largely unsuccessful, and global CO 2

levels continue to rise (Figure 2a). 12 It is important to note that Figure 2a represents annual CO 2 emissions, not the cumulative concentration of CO 2 in the atmosphere, meaning that the amount of CO 2 being emitted each year is increasing. Interestingly, during the COVID-19 pandemic near 2020, a temporary decline in economic activity has been reported to coincide with a modest decrease in CO 2 emissions. 12 −14 

However, emissions quickly rebounded as the pandemic subsided, underscoring the strong link between human activity and CO 2 emissions. This trend also highlights the failure of humanity in making progress in mitigating CO 2 emissions. The Paris Agreement in 2015 sets a long-term goal of holding the increase in the global average temperature to well below 2 °Cabove preindustrial levels and pursuing efforts to limit the increase to 1.5 °C. 15 According to the IPCC Special Report on Global Warming of 1.5 °C, pathways consistent with limiting warming to 1.5 °C imply that global CO 2 emissions decline about 45% by 2030 and reach net zero around 2050. 16 

However, if the current trajectory of CO 2 emissions persists, the remaining carbon budget compatible with limiting warming to 1.5 °C could be exhausted before 2030, reducing the likelihood of avoiding the severe impacts of climate change. 17 Despite abundant evidence, some countries remain skeptical about global warming. Nevertheless, only in recent years have national efforts begun to intensify in addressing CO 2 reduction on a broader scale. Figure 2a shows that most of CO 2 is emitted from largely three sectors; power, industry, and ground transport. One of the most widely invested CO 2 mitigation technologies is Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage (CCUS). CCUS is a suite of technologies designed to capture CO 2 emissions from industrial processes or power plants, and either store it underground or utilize it for various applications (e.g., producing green methanol). While the key advantages and future challenges of CCUS have been extensively reviewed in recent studies, 18 −20 and thus will not be described in detail here, it is important to highlight that CCUS is well-suited for large-scale CO 2 emissions, especially from industries and power plants that generate highly concentrated CO 2 emissions in localized areas. CCUS technology is inherently large and heavy because it is designed to handle massive volumes of CO 2, requiring complex infrastructure for high-pressure processing, compression, and integration into large industrial facilities. For this reason, while CCUS is effective in reducing CO 2 emissions from power and industrial sectors, where concentrated CO 2 streams (concentration ≥ 10%) are emitted at fixed locations, it is not suitable for vehicles (Figure 2c). Vehicles have limited space and weight capacity to accommodate CCUS equipment, and the energy required to capture CO 2 from dilute vehicle emissions is impractical. This underscores the need for alternative CO 2 mitigation strategy in the transportation sector, the third-largest source of global CO 2 emissions. A promising strategy for reducing CO 2 emissions from the transportation sector is the transition from fuel-based ICVs to EVs. EVs broadly include both battery EVs (BEVs) and fuel cell EVs (FCEVs). BEVs operate using the electricity stored in rechargeable onboard batteries, whereas FCEVs produce electricity onboard through the electrochemical conversion of hydrogen in a fuel cell. Among these, BEVs are currently more economical and account for the dominant share of the market. 21 Therefore, the present study will mainly focus on BEVs. Because EVs rely on grid electricity for charging, their emissions reduction potential depends on the electricity generation mix, which still includes a substantial contribution from fossil fuels globally ( ∼60%). 21,22 Even so, EV adoption remains an environmentally viable solution for several reasons. First, CO 2 emissions associated with EV use are more manageable than those from ICVs because electricity is produced at fixed facilities where CCUS technologies can be used, unlike vehicle exhaust emissions, which are released from millions of dispersed sources (Figure 2c). Also, the renewable electricity generation is increasing rapidly. Over the past decade, global renewable power generation has grown by more than 50% and now accounts for ∼40% of total electricity production (Figure S2). 7 As electricity grids become cleaner, the carbon footprint of EVs will continue to decline. Finally, unlike ICVs, EVs produce no tailpipe emissions of CO or NO x,thereby providing important air-quality benefits. Taken together, EVs offer a promising pathway for addressing both climate change and air pollution in the transportation sector. However, despite their significant environmental advantages over ICVs, EVs are not yet acomplete replacement. Several challenges remain before their widespread adoption can be achieved. Importantly, these challenges extend beyond technological limitations. The pace of transition is also shaped by policy support, investment confidence, and broader market conditions. Hence, alongside technological considerations, it is important to understand the current market landscape and the structural barriers affecting EV adoption. In this context, the next section examines the benefits and drawbacks of EVs in greater detail to provide a comprehensive view of their role in the future of sustainable transportation. 

4. EV TRANSITION: BENEFITS, CHALLENGES, AND THE ROAD AHEAD 

4.1. Advantage of EVs in Terms of Its Cleanness and Price 

In order to understand why EVs are regarded as a greener and more viable alternative to conventional ICVs in the trans-portation sector, it would be essential to first examine their key advantages, especially from an environmental perspective. First of all, EVs produce not only lower CO 2 emissions but also in a more controllable manner compared to ICVs. Figure 3a compares the amount of CO 2 emissions and their sources from EVs and ICVs, evaluated in 2021 for different vehicle sizes over their life cycle. 23 In the case of ICVs, the largest share of CO 2 emissions comes from the tailpipe: 31 tCO 2e, 32 tCO 2e, and 43 tCO 2e for small, medium, and large ICVs, respectively, accounting for 63%, 58%, and 65% of total CO 2

emissions. This is expected as these vehicles derive their energy from burning carbon-based fossil fuels. The CO 2 emissions from battery manufacturing, fuel/electricity production, and vehicle manufacturing are relatively small for ICVs. On the other hand, EVs have no tailpipe CO 2 emissions (as they do not run on fossil fuels), leading to a significant reduction in total CO 2 emissions: from 49 to 38 tCO 2e for small vehicles (22% reduction), from 55 to 40 tCO 2e for medium vehicles (27% reduction), and from 66 to 45 tCO 2e for large vehicles (32% reduction). 4.2. “Chasm” in EV Adoption 

Despite the numerous advantages of EVs, the year-over-year growth rate of new EV passenger car sales has been steadily declining over the past four years, from 109% in 2021 to 56.9% in 2022, 33.4% in 2023, and further down to 23.7% in 2024 (note that the 2024 data include only sales from January to October) (Figure 5a). 28 This decline in EV sales growth is observed worldwide, regardless of region (Figure S3). 21,29 

While the EV market is undoubtedly expanding, the slowing growth rate suggests that certain demerits discourage consumers from purchasing EVs, despite their benefits. Understanding the reasons behind this trend, often referred to as “chasm” in EV adoption, is important. Several factors have been cited as contributing to this chasm in EV adoption, including limited charging infrastructure, range anxiety, high costs, and safety concerns. However, these challenges are being rapidly addressed. For instance, although the number of EV charging stations remains insufficient, it has increased more than 10-fold over the past eight years (Figure S4). 21 Similarly, advances in battery technology have substantially extended the driving range of EVs, with the average range increasing from 191 km in 2015 to 339 km in 2024 (Figure S5). 21 In the United States, the maximum range of EVs available for sale reached 516 km in 2024. 30 Recent data also dispel the perception that electric vehicles pose greater safety risks, particularly regarding fire incidents. In fact, multiple studies demonstrate that EVs catch fire far less frequently than ICVs. 31,32 For example, data compiled by the U.S. National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) and the Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS) indicate that EVs experience ∼25 fires per 100,000 vehicles, compared with 

∼1530 fires per 100,000 gasoline-powered vehicles (Figure S6). 31 

Thus, many of the initial concerns regarding EVs are being rapidly addressed, and in several areas, such as safety, EVs may be outperforming ICVs. However, it is important to recognize that EVs remain a relatively new technology, and media coverage could amplify concerns, sometimes exaggerating the risks associated with EVs. This can contribute to a public perception of increased risk, even when data suggests the opposite. Nonetheless, regardless of how long it takes, considering the rapid pace of technological advancements and infrastructure development, it is likely that this chasm will be overcome, and EVs will gradually replace ICVs as the dominant vehicle type in the market, as projected in several reports (Figure S7). 21,33,34 

4.3. Hybrid EVs as a Response to the Chasm 

In response to the chasm, hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs) and plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEVs), which combine a traditional ICV with an electric motor and battery to improve fuel efficiency and reduce emissions, are gaining popular-ity. 35,36 An HEV combines a gasoline engine with an electric motor but does not require external charging, as it recharges through regenerative braking and engine power. In contrast, a PHEV operates similarly but has a larger battery, allowing it to drive short distances (typically 20 −100 km) on electricity alone before switching to gasoline; it requires external charging for optimal efficiency. These hybrid EVs offer several advantages, including reduced dependency on charging infrastructure, improved fuel efficiency, lower CO 2 emissions, and a driving experience that remains familiar to ICV users. As a result, the market share of hybrid EVs is growing rapidly, currently surpassing that of fully EVs (denoted as BEVs). For example, in 2024, HEVs accounted for ∼11% of light-duty vehicle sales, while PHEVs made up ∼2%. Combined, the market share of hybrid EVs in light-duty vehicle sales reached 

∼13%, exceeding the ∼7% share of BEVs (Figure 5b). 36 

However, while hybrid EVs serve as a transitional solution to bridge the gap in EV adoption, it is crucial to acknowledge that they cannot serve as a permanent replacement for ICVs. Hybrid EVs still emit a significant amount of CO 2 and air pollutants, in some cases even more than traditional ICVs. 4.4. The Challenge of Electrifying Heavy Trucks 

The adoption of electric heavy-duty vehicles (HDVs), such as heavy trucks and buses, faces major challenges due to the large battery weight and its impact on operational efficiency. 2,21 

Unlike diesel-powered HDVs that rely on relatively lightweight fuel tanks, electric trucks require much heavier battery packs to achieve comparable driving ranges. For instance, while diesel trucks store energy in fuel tanks weighing a few hundred kg, electric trucks need packs weighing several tonnes, substan-tially increasing vehicle mass and reducing allowable payload. 37 

This added weight limits cargo capacity under legal weight limits, lowering efficiency and raising transport costs. Due to these limitations, the adoption rate of zero-emission heavy-duty vehicles remains much lower than that of passenger EVs, where market shares often exceed 10% in leading markets. In the EU, for example, heavy trucks (weighing over 12 tonnes) accounted for 77% of all HDV sales in the first quarter of 2024, with 67000 units sold. However, out of these, only 750 (1.1%) were zero-emission vehicles, demonstrating the slow adoption of electric trucks (Figure 6). 38 In contrast, medium-duty trucks or light HDVs (weighing between 3.5 and 12 tonnes) had a higher zero-emission market share of 8%, reflecting that weight-related challenges are easier to overcome in lighter commercial vehicles. 38 Unlike the chasm in EV adoption seen in passenger vehicles, where consumer hesitancy is a major barrier, the electrification of heavy trucks faces fundamental technical challenges that will require significant breakthroughs in battery technology and lightweight materials. Given that the full electrification of heavy trucks will take a longer time, catalysis researchers will have an important role to play in developing solutions to mitigate the environmental impact of diesel fuel, which will continue to be used in freight transport for the foreseeable future. In summary, EVs offer significant environmental benefits by reducing CO 2 emissions and improving air quality compared to ICVs. Since EVs do not produce tailpipe emissions, they contribute to lower overall carbon emissions while also minimizing harmful air pollutants. Additionally, as EV technology continues to advance, their total cost of ownership has become increasingly competitive with ICVs, primarily due to lower fuel and maintenance costs. However, despite these advantages, EV adoption has recently slowed, reflecting a market resistance often referred to as the chasm in EV adoption. Concerns over charging infrastructure, battery range, or safety perceptions continue to impact consumer confidence, driving increased interest in hybrid EVs as transitional solutions. Meanwhile, electrification in the HDV sector faces even greater challenges, as battery weight and range limitations significantly hinder the efficiency of freight transport. As a result, while EVs are gradually replacing ICVs, this transition is expected to take at least a decade or more. This means that ICVs will remain an essential technology for a considerable period, making it crucial for automotive catalysis researchers to understand these market trends. A deep understanding of these transitions will enable researchers to effectively develop and optimize catalytic technologies suited for specific applications and future needs. In the following section, we will discuss the technical limitations of hybrid EVs, which are currently being proposed as an alternative technology to bridge the adoption gap, to further clarify the future direction of automotive catalysis research. 

5. HYBRID EVS AS A BRIDGE TO ELECTRIFICATION: BENEFITS AND EMISSIONS RISKS 

5.1. Hybrid EVs as a Transitional Technology Bridging the Gap toward Full EV Adoption 

As discussed in the previous section, hybrid EVs currently command a larger market share than battery EVs, illustrating their role as a widely accepted intermediate technology in the mass adoption of fully EVs (Figure 5b). 39 

The operational CO 2 emissions of hybrid EVs are significantly lower than those of ICVs by approximately 25%, although they remain approximately 30% higher than those of EVs (Figure S8). 40 Notably, hybrid EVs reduce operational CO 2 emissions by partially relying on battery-stored energy, leading to lower fossil fuel consumption during driving. Within the hybrid segment, PHEVs generally show lower CO 2

emissions than conventional HEVs, as they can operate more frequently in electric-only mode due to their larger battery capacity. 5.2. Hybrid EVs May Emit More Air Pollutants than ICVs due to Frequent Cold Starts 

While hybrid EVs are recognized as a bridging technology in overcoming the chasm toward EV adoption, one critical environmental challenge deserves attention: in certain conditions, hybrid EVs can emit higher levels of air pollutants than ICVs. At first glance, this may seem counterintuitive, as hybrid EVs typically consume less fossil fuel and are associated with lower CO 2 emissions. However, this paradox arises from the frequent cold-start cycles that occur during hybrid vehicle operation, particularly in urban driving environments. Cold-start emissions refer to the elevated release of pollutants from ICVs when both the engine and the catalytic converter are below their optimal operating temperature, typically immediately after engine ignition. During this period, incomplete combustion and the reduced efficiency of after-treatment systems result in high emissions of CO, NO x,unburned HC, and PM. A significant fraction of an ICV’s total emissions can occur during this cold-start phase, especially during short trips or in stop-and-go driving conditions common to urban environments. For gasoline vehicles, approximately 40 −60% of total NO x emissions, 60 −80% of total CO emissions, 50 −70% of HC emissions, and 50 −70% of PM emissions are known to occur during the cold-start period. 46 −48 Diesel vehicles, while exhibiting a different emission profile, also show elevated cold-start emissions, accounting for approximately 20 −40% of total NO x, 30 −50% of CO, 30 −50% of HC, and 40 −60% of PM emissions during this phase. 49 Hybrid vehicles face a particular challenge with cold-start emissions due to their dual powertrain operation. Hence, as hybrid EVs will continue to serve as a transitional technology toward full electrification, mitigating cold-start emissions will be essential to maximizing their environmental benefits. 

5.3. Technologies to Reduce Cold-Start Air Pollutant Emissions 

Since cold-start emissions account for a substantial share of total air pollutant emissions from ICVs, mitigating them has become a critical challenge. In response, two categories of mitigation strategies have emerged: engineering-based and catalyst-based solutions. Therefore, leveraging these estab-lished technologies would offer promising pathways for reducing cold-start emissions in HEVs and addressing the associated air pollution challenges. Engineering approaches aim to reduce cold-start emissions by shortening the cold-start period through faster heating of engine and exhaust system components. Commonly applied strategies include electrically heated catalysts (EHC), secon-dary air injection (SAI), and exhaust heat recovery systems (EHRS), all designed to accelerate the warming of exhaust gases and catalyst substrates during cold-start period. In parallel, catalytic solutions aim to enhance the efficiency of after-treatment systems under the low-temperature con-ditions typical of cold-starts. A significant focus of research has been on mitigating NO x emissions from diesel engines, where excess oxygen during lean-burn combustion significantly limits the effectiveness of NO x control technologies. The Dieselgate scandal is a prime example of how challenging NO x control is in diesel engines. One of the approaches developed to address cold-start NO x emissions is the Passive NO x Adsorber (PNA), which captures NO x at low exhaust temperatures (<100 °C) and releases it downstream for reduction in the SCR system once the latter is thermally activated (>200 °C). 52 −55 In contrast, for gasoline engines, cold-start NO x emissions are typically less severe due to stoichiometric combustion and the high efficiency of TWCs. Beyond NO x control, catalytic research has also targeted PM and HC emissions, which remain challenges for both diesel and gasoline vehicles under cold-start conditions. Similar to the concept of PNAs, cold-start HC traps and diesel particulate filters (DPFs) have been studied as strategies to mitigate HC and PM emissions during this thermally constrained period. 52 

These technologies originally developed to mitigate cold-start emissions from ICVs can be adapted to hybrid EVs to address their cold-start challenges. As discussed in Section 5.1, most hybrid EVs currently rely on gasoline engines, making the reduction of HC and PM emissions particularly important. Moreover, although cold-start NO x emissions from gasoline engines have historically received less attention than those from diesel engines, the issue is now drawing increasing attention. With increasingly stringent NO x regulations (Figure S11) and the growing number of gasoline vehicles, including gasoline-based hybrids, mitigating cold-start NO x emissions is expected to become an even more pressing concern. Consequently, further development of gasoline-specific tech-nologies, such as PNA analogues, will be essential. 56 −58 

In summary, hybrid EVs, particularly gasoline-based hybrids, are emerging as a key transitional technology that bridges the gap between ICVs and EVs, helping to accelerate EV adoption. Nonetheless, a critical environmental challenge persists: frequent cold-starts in urban driving, caused by the repeated cycling of the engine on and off, impair the catalytic converter’s ability to effectively reduce emissions of NO x, CO, HC, and PM. To mitigate this issue, cold-start emission control technologies developed for diesel and gasoline ICVs, such as electrically heated catalysts or passive NO x adsorbers, may need to be adapted for hybrid EVs. Deploying these solutions will be essential to addressing environmental concerns and enabling the broader adoption of hybrid EVs as a bridge toward full electrification of transportation sector. 

6. RESEARCH AGENDA FOR AUTOMOTIVE CATALYSTS IN THE ERA OF VEHICLE ELECTRIFICATION

As shown in Section 4, EVs generate substantially lower CO 2 and pollutant emissions, providing a clear environmental advantage over gasoline- or diesel-based ICVs. Furthermore, recent techno-

logical developments have reduced the total cost of ownership for EVs, making them more economical than ICVs (Section 4.1). These factors highlight a strong impetus for the transition to EVs. Despite this momentum, market data suggests that the shift from ICVs to EVs may take longer than initially expected. Several challenges, such as limited charging infrastructure, range anxiety, battery degradation concerns, and safety questions, have slowed EV market growth, a phenomenon referred to as the “chasm” in EV adoption (Section 4.2). In addition, heavy-duty trucks face a significant technological hurdle in switching to EVs due to the weight and capacity constraints of current battery technology (Section 4.4). As a result, heavy-duty trucks will likely continue to rely on ICVs unless significant breakthroughs occur. In an effort to bridge the adoption gap, hybrid EVs have gained attention (Sections 4.3 and 5.1). By merging an electric powertrain with a conventional ICVs, hybrid EVs alleviate some of the most pressing concerns associated with EVs. However, although hybrid vehicles emit less CO 2 than ICVs, they can produce higher levels of certain air pollutants due to frequent cold-start events. In these conditions, automotive catalysts do not reach optimal operating temperature, resulting in ineffective pollutant conversion (Section 5.2). These market trends offer valuable insights for the automotive catalysis community. As noted in Sections 1 and 4.1, it may be unrealistic for automotive catalysts to remain as dominant as they once were, given the increasing share of EVs (Figure S7). Nevertheless, their role remains vital during this transitional period, which could span several decades due to persistent adoption barriers and technological limitations. First, given the advantages of EVs (Section 4.1), it seems prudent to avoid large-scale new investments in catalyst technology. Several market projections indicate that the share of ICVs will continue to decline and is unlikely to rebound. Still, because ICVs will be in use for decades to come, particularly for heavy-duty applications, exhaust catalyst research must be both strategic and efficient. Rather than broad, exhaustive research, efforts should concentrate on “smart” innovations that enhance catalytic performance. Examples include refining mature technologies for improved efficiency and leveraging AI-driven materials screening to accelerate the discovery of new catalyst formulations. Indeed, recent studies indicate that simply adjusting the gasoline engine operation can enhance the performance of aftertreat-ment systems, even during the cold-start period. 56,59 The strength of this approach lies in its distinction from conventional catalyst research; this study integrates engine functionality with catalyst performance to develop the technology in a more streamlined manner. This example shows that, even without exhaustive catalyst development, leveraging well-established technologies can effectively mitigate air pollutants emitted from ICVs. Such holistic approaches will prove crucial for an industry destined to shrink over the coming decades. In parallel, the rise of hybrid EVs as an interim solution underscores the importance of mitigating cold-start emissions. The catalysis community’s extensive experience in controlling NO x, HC, PM, and CO can be adapted for hybrid systems. However, these strategies must be optimized for gasoline-based exhaust conditions, given the market shift away from diesel and the growing popularity of gasoline-based EVs. Gasoline exhaust typically contains a dynamic mix of reducing gases (e.g., CO, H2, HC) and oxidizing species (e.g., O 2, H 2O), which can hinder the performance of existing cold-start technologies designed for diesel engines. For instance, Pd-zeolite PNAs, effective under lean diesel conditions, can lose their NO x

storage capability in a partially reducing environment where Pd ions aggregate into inactive clusters. 60 Accordingly, new or adapted catalyst systems must address these unique gasoline exhaust challenges. Finally, because electrifying HDVs remains difficult, it is also crucial to explore catalytic solutions that enable net-zero CO 2

emissions for trucks and other commercial applications. Many alternative technologies aim to decarbonize HDVs by replacing diesel with “clean” fuels. Efforts include H2-fueled ICVs, ammonia (NH 3)-fueled ICVs, H 2 fuel cells, e-fuels, among others. 35,61 −63 The catalyst community will play a pivotal role in advancing these technologies. Catalytic processes are essential for the production of H 2 or NH 3 (via, for example, H2O splitting or N 2−H2 reactions) as well as e-fuels (by reacting CO 2 with H 2). Furthermore, even with clean fuels, engines can still emit pollutants such as NO x, CO, HC, and PM, underscoring the ongoing need for effective aftertreatment systems. Importantly, the composition of exhaust pollutants will vary depending on the fuel type, requiring tailored catalyst formulations to optimize performance. 62 −64 These evolving challenges present valuable new opportunities for the automotive catalyst community. In conclusion, although automotive catalysts are not used directly in EVs, they will remain critically important in a market that continues to rely on ICVs, especially hybrids and HDVs. By understanding these shifting market trends and pursuing timely, focused research, the automotive catalysis field can retain its relevance and make an even greater contribution to sustainable mobility by contributing to mitigating CO 2 and air pollutant emissions. We hope that this work will serve as a guide, helping the automotive catalysis community chart a clear course toward a cleaner and more sustainable transportation sector.
