ABSTRACT: Herbicide and nitrate pollution frequently co-occur in coastal waters, while their combined effect on corals has been a critical knowledge gap. Although corals can exhibit acclimation to nitrate enrichment, it is unclear whether this acclimation persists under coexposure to PSII herbicides. Here, we investigated the combined effects of nitrate and the PSII herbicide prometryn on the reef-building coral Acropora hyacinthus by integrating physiology, ultrastructure, and host-symbiont multiomics. The results demon-strate that prometryn acts as the primary driver of photodamage and photosynthetic toxicity in the symbiotic Symbiodiniaceae under costress. Transmission electron microscopy revealed that prometryn induced activated symbiophagy and chloroplast degradation with loosely stacked thylakoids in Symbiodiniaceae. Transcriptomics and proteomics analyses predicted that nitrate enrichment stimulated host glutathione-based antioxidant responses. However, prometryn-induced phototoxicity impaired photosynthate supply and disrupted this nitrate acclimation mechanism. Moreover, reduced photosynthate transfer further impaired the host energy balance and intensified oxidative stress. These findings show that PSII herbicides can override short-term acclimation to nitrate enrichment, underscoring the need for the joint regulation of herbicide and nutrient inputs in reef ecosystems. 

KEYWORDS: nitrate, PSII herbicide, prometryn, coral, symbiodiniaceae 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Coral reefs are ecologically and economically vital, underpinning marine biodiversity, fisheries, and coastal protection. 1 The endosymbiosis between reef-building corals and Symbiodinia-ceae forms the foundation of coral reefs’ ecological success. However, coral reef degradation has intensified in recent decades, primarily driven by the cumulative impact of anthropogenic stressors such as overfishing, global climate change, and environmental pollution. 2 Notably, the co-occurrence of nitrogen enrichment and PSII herbicides poses a severe threat to coral reefs. 3,4 

Nutrient pollution is a common local stressor on coral reefs and has been implicated in the significant loss of coral cover and diversity. 5 The input of anthropogenic reactive nitrogen into the global biosphere is increasing rapidly, primarily from nitrogen fertilizers; 6 other major sources include municipal sewage, industrial effluent, andaquaculture. 7 Large quantities of reactive nitrogen are transported to the oceans via river systems or atmospheric deposition. 8,9 The tropics are projected to experience significant increases in nitrogen inputs and land-use changes, which will elevate nitrogen losses to coastal oceans and the atmosphere. 10 This trend is already evident in some local coastal waters of Hainan Island, where nutrient pollution remains a pressing environmental issue. 11 At several sampling sites in reef-proximal areas, dissolved inorganic nitrogen (DIN) concentrations have been recorded as high as 26.85 μmol/ L, 12 alevel that far exceeds the threshold for coral survival ( ≤9 μmol/ L). 13 Such elevated nitrogen loading exacerbates marine eutrophication, adversely affecting marine ecosystems and biodiversity. 14 These impacts are particularly severe for sensitive coral reef ecosystems. For instance, nitrate (NO 3−) enrichment can increase the retention of photosynthetic products by Symbiodiniaceae, thereby destabilizing the coral −algae symbio-sis. 5,15,16 

Recently, pesticides have become pervasive environmental pollutants, representing a global threat to coral reef ecosystems. 17 −19 Of the 0.94 Tg net annual pesticide input in 2015, approximately 0.71 Gg of active ingredients are released into the oceans each year, with herbicides accounting for the

More than half of these herbicides are photosynthetic inhibitors that can significantly impact primary productivity and zooplankton globally at very low concentrations. 20 Upon entering the ocean, these PSII herbicides bind to the QB site of the D1 protein in PSII, thereby inhibiting photosynthesis in Symbiodiniaceae and inducing oxidative damage that may ultimately lead to coral bleaching. 21 −23 Global monitoring data from 1990 to 2022 indicate that triazine herbicide concen-trations in coastal waters exhibit substantial spatiotemporal heterogeneity. 20 While median total triazine levels in most bays range from 0.01 to 0.64 μg/L, episodic runoff events frequently generate peak concentrations exceeding 1 μg/L. For instance, total triazine concentrations as high as approximately 6 μg/L and 3 μg/L have been reported in Thermaikos Gulf and the Gulf of Mexico, respectively. Notably, diuron and atrazine were measured within the Great Barrier Reef (GBR) at around 1.2 

μg/L during the period of 2017 −2018. 22 Similarly, prometryn concentrations of up to 0.4 μg/L have been reported in waters surrounding Hainan Island, indicating the potential for even higher transient exposures near agricultural discharge points. 23 

Collectively, these data indicate that a concentration of 1 μg/L likely represents the upper bound of chronic prometryn exposure in contaminated reef areas, whereas 5 μg/L corresponds to an acute pulse scenario that may occur during extreme runoff events. Furthermore, our preliminary studies determined that the lowest observed effect concentration (LOEC) of prometryn on the effective quantum yield [Y(II)] of Acropora hyacinthus is 1.0 μg/L, with a half-maximal effect concentration (EC 50 ) of 4.7 μg/L. 24 

Agricultural intensification elevates nitrate and PSII herbicide loads in reef waters, amplifying ecological risks. 25 At environ-mentally relevant concentrations, PSII herbicide prometryn can sensitize even stress-tolerant corals to ocean warming. 3

Moreover, nitrogen pollution can increase the susceptibility of reef corals to bleaching. 16,26 Notably, corals can exhibit short-term acclimation to nitrate enrichment at environmentally relevant levels, with increased Symbiodiniaceae density and chlorophyll but no immediate photophysiological impairment or bleaching. 24 It remains unclear whether PSII herbicide exposure disrupts this acclimatory response, potentially exacerbating the susceptibility of corals to nitrate enrichment when the stressors co-occur. In our previous work, short-term nitrate enrichment alone was associated with a return of coral photophysiology and oxidative stress markers to baseline, whereas coexposure to nitrate and PSII herbicide prometryn led to greater photosystem impairment and suppression of antioxidant defenses. 24 We hypothesized that prometryn would induce Symbiodiniaceae phototoxicity and compromise the short-term acclimation of corals to nitrate enrichment. To test this, we conducted 2- and 9-day exposure experiments using bleaching-sensitive reef-building coral A. hyacinthus .27 Acombination of physiological, ultrastructural, and host-symbiont transcriptomic and proteomic analyses was employed to explore the mechanisms underlying the effects of PSII herbicides and nitrate coexposure on coral health. These findings provide preliminary clarification of the potential mechanisms of PSII herbicides and nitrate coexposure in corals and underscore the need to consider multiple agricultural pollutants in reef risk assessments. 

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS 

2.1. Coral Preparation and Exposure Design 

Based on our previous field survey, which identified prometryn as a predominant PSII herbicide (up to 440 ng/L) in Hainan coastal waters, 23 we selected it as a model herbicide to investigate its combined effects with nitrate enrichment on A. hyacinthus . The nitrogen concentration settings in our experiments were referenced from the study by Duprey et al. 13 They found the coverage of living reef corals to be lowest when the DIN enrichment in seawater exceeded 9 μmol/L. In preliminary dose −response experiments, the LOEC and the EC 50 were determined for prometryn (1.0 and 4.7 μg/L, respectively) and nitrate (5.7 μmol/L) using the Y(II) of Symbiodiniaceae as the endpoint. 24 

The detailed procedures for this part of the experiment have been fully described in Text S1. Based on these toxicity thresholds and their environmental relevance, final exposure concentrations of 9 μmol/L nitrate and 1 or 5 μg/L prometryn were chosen for the main experiment. A colony of A. hyacinthus was collected from an open sea area in Wanning, Hainan Province, China, and acclimated it in a filtered seawater circulation aquarium. Detailed conditions for coral acclimation are provided in Text S1. After acclimation, corals were then exposed to either 1 μL/L dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, CK), 1 μg/L prometryn (P1), 5 μg/L prometryn (P5), 9 μmol/L nitrate (N9), or a combination of nitrate and prometryn enrichment (NP1 and NP5) in the 5 L test solution. The final DMSO concentration in each treatment was set to 1 μL/L, which is 1 order of magnitude lower than the LOEC value reported in previous studies. 28 The exposure experiment was terminated on day 9 due to bleaching observed in the NP5 treatment group. Physiological/biochemical indices were measured on days 0, 2, and 9, with coral fragments destructively sampled at each of these three time points. Furthermore, coral fragments were collected at 2 days (T1) and 9 days (T2), flash-frozen in liquid N 2, and stored at −80 °C for RNA and protein extraction. Seawater salinity, irradiance, and photoperiod (12 h light:12 h dark) were maintained identically across all treatments. The exposure medium was renewed daily with fresh seawater over the 9-day experimental period. Furthermore, to determine the optimal dosing interval for prometryn, its real-time concentration was monitored at 6-h intervals (0, 6, 12, 18, 24 h) in the P1, P5, NP1, and NP5 treatments, which informed the replenishment schedule (Figure S1A). To ensure stable exposure concentrations, nitrate and prometryn were delivered via an automated dosing pump, maintaining nominal concentrations of 

∼9 μmol/L and ∼1 μg/L (or ∼5 μg/L for P5/NP5), respectively, in their respective treatments. The concentrations of both analytes in the exposure medium were verified on days 2, 4, 6, and 9 (Figure S1B −C). Nitrate was quantified by the zinc/cadmium reduction colorimetric method. Seawater prometryn was preconcentrated using OASIS HLB solid-phase extraction cartridges (6 mL, 200 mg). For coral tissues, prometryn was quantified separately in hosts and Symbiodiniaceae after extraction with acetonitrile/acetic acid (99:1, v/v). Prometryn was determined by LC −MS/MS (AB Sciex QTRAP 6500+). Detailed analytical procedures for prometryn and nitrate are provided in Text S2. 

2.2. Physiological Parameter Analysis 

The physico-biochemical indicators were determined to assess photosynthetic damage and oxidative stress in corals. Host tissues and symbiotic dinoflagellates were separated according to our previously published protocol. 29 Methods for quantifying chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, chlorophyll a and c2 , Symbiodiniaceae density, NADPH/NADP +, glutathione S-transferase (GST) activity, glutathione (GSH), and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) are provided in Text S3. 

2.3. Transmission Electron Microscopy Imaging 

Coral tissues were processed for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) following standard procedures. 30 Briefly, fragments were initially fixed in 5 mL of TEM fixative comprising 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde prepared in Instant Ocean artificial seawater (IO) for 3 days. 2.4. Transcriptome Analysis and RT-qPCR Validation 

Total RNA from coral holobionts was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and assessed for integrity and purity with a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA) and a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA). Libraries were prepared and sequenced by Majorbio Technology (Shanghai, China); platform and protocol details are provided in Texts S4. After filtering the raw sequencing data to obtain clean reads, the next critical step was to differentiate between the coral host sequences and those of the Symbiodiniaceae. Prior to host −symbiont read partitioning, Symbiodiniaceae community composition was profiled by sequencing. Community structure remained stable at days 2 and 9 and was dominated by Symbiodinium goreaui (Clade C1). 24 Coral host-and Symbiodiniaceae-derived reads were then separated using BBsplit v38.90 under default parameters, with species-specific reference genomes for the coral host (https://marinegenomics.oist.jp/ahya/ viewer/info?project_id=91) and Symbiodiniaceae (http://symbs. reefgenomics.org/download/). Gene expression levels in coral hosts and Symbiodiniaceae were quantified separately using RSEM software. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using |log2(fold change) | ≥ 2 and p ≤ 0.05, and DEGs were annotated to KEGG Orthology (KO) terms. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was used to validate the RNA sequencing results. Detailed steps and specific primers were provided in Text S5 and Table S5, respectively.

2.6. Statistical Analyses 

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 19.0; IBM, USA) and R (version 4.3.3). Differences in prometryn concentration between the coral host and Symbiodiniaceae within the control and exposure groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test. To examine the interaction effect of prometryn and nitrate stress, a two-way ANOVA was conducted, with Tukey’s HSD test applied for post hoc pairwise comparisons. For data sets where the assumption of equal variance was violated, a general linear model was fitted, and heteroskedasticity was assessed using White’s test. The significance threshold was set at p ≤

0.05 for all tests. Assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were verified prior to analysis. Data visualization was performed in OriginPro 2021 (OriginLab) and R (v4.3.3). Results are reported as mean ±

standard deviation (SD). To assess the relationship between DEGs and DEPs, a two-way orthogonal partial least-squares (O2PLS) model was implemented with the OmicsPLS package in R. 32 Details of the proteomic and transcriptomic analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA) and KEGG, GO, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) enrichment, are provided in Text S3.

Y(II) (Figure 1A). However, at an environmentally relevant prometryn level, the nitrate LOEC for YII decreased from 5.7 to 4.5 μmol/L (Figure 1A), indicating increased nitrate sensitivity under coexposure. On day 2 of exposure, prometryn had already accumulated in both coral host cells and Symbiodiniaceae. As the exposure period extended to 9 days, the accumulated concentrations gradually increased, with higher accumulation observed in Symbiodiniaceae than in host cells (Figure 1B). This may be attributed to the presence of the QB site on the D1 protein, the target site of PSII herbicides, 33 in the Symbiodiniaceae. Additionally, prometryn is a moderately lipophilic organic molecule, 33 and the abundant thylakoid membranes in Symbiodiniaceae facilitate its absorption and accumulation. 34 Furthermore, after 9 days of exposure, the accumulated concentration of prometryn in Symbiodiniaceae in the NP1 costress treatment (172.41 ± 5.15 ng/g, p < 0.05) group was significantly higher than that in the P1 treatment group (141.30 ± 5.96 ng/g) (Figure 1B). This pattern is consistent with nutrient-driven remodeling of thylakoid membranes: under high-N or low-P conditions, Symbiodinia-ceae experience phosphorus starvation, substitute phospholipids with nonphosphorus lipids, and thereby alter membrane physicochemical properties in ways that can increase the partitioning of moderately lipophilic organic molecules into the membrane phase. 35 

After 9 days, both prometryn alone (1 and 5 μg/L) and nitrate-prometryn coexposure significantly reduced Symbiodi-niaceae chlorophyll a (p < 0.05; Figure 1C). NADPH levels also decreased significantly under environmentally relevant prome-tryn and costress treatments ( p < 0.05; Figure 1D). Chlorophyll 

a is the primary pigment in the light-harvesting antenna complexes and photosystem I (PSI) supercomplexes, forming part of the structural basis for directing excitation energy toward the reaction centers. 36 In oxygenic photosynthesis, chlorophyll a

located within the reaction centers initiates the primary charge separation process and drives electron transport. 37 During linear electron flow, ferredoxin-NADP + reductase (FNR) catalyzes the final reduction of NADP + to form NADPH. 38 TEM revealed no discernible ultrastructural damage in Symbiodiniaceae under nitrate enrichment alone. In contrast, prometryn (1 and 5 μg/L) and prometryn-nitrate costress induced chloroplast damage characterized by loosely stacked thylakoids (Figures 2C and S2E), implicating prometryn as the primary driver of ultra-structural injury under coexposure. Consistent with functional and structural impairment, two-way ANOVA also detected a significant interaction for Symbiodiniaceae lipid ( p < 0.05; Table S2), in line with membrane perturbation under costress. A clear vacuolar matrix was observed in both the prometryn-only and costress groups (Figures 2C and S2A). The pronounced vacuolization and vacuolar matrices observed in Symbiodinia-ceae are consistent with ultrastructural hallmarks of symbioph-agy and lysosomal degradation of symbionts reported during bleaching. 39 −41 Such autophagic removal of compromised Symbiodiniaceae is a common component of the cnidarian stress response and bleaching. 39 −41 Such selective removal of compromised symbionts may explain the significant decline in Symbiodiniaceae density observed here ( p < 0.05; Figure 1E). In summary, our results indicate that prometryn reduces symbiotic Symbiodiniaceae density primarily by inducing phototoxicity ( p

< 0.05; Figure 1E). Our previous study also demonstrated that prometryn exposure significantly decreased photosynthetic performance, as evidenced by reductions in Fv /Fm (maximum quantum yield of PSII), thereby providing independent support for this conclusion. 3,24 In contrast, 9 days of nitrate enrichment did not measurably affect the physiology of the Symbiodinia-ceae. Furthermore, a significant increase in GST activity accompanied by a marked decrease in the reduced-to-oxidized glutathione (GSH/GSSG) ratio was observed under costress conditions (Figure 2A −B). This indicates that combined stress may disrupt the intracellular redox homeostasis in Symbiodi-niaceae. Correlation analysis revealed that under combined stress of nitrate enrichment and 1 μg/mL prometryn, NADPH levels in Symbiodiniaceae showed a significant positive correlation with GST activity, but a significant negative correlation with both the GSH/GSSG ratio and chlorophyll a

content ( p < 0.05; Figure S3). NADPH and oxygen serve as two major sinks for electrons escaping from Photosystem I (PSI). 42 

The reduction of oxygen by photosynthetic electrons leads to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby inducing oxidative stress. 43 

Two-way ANOVA revealed significant interactions between prometryn and nitrate for host NADP +, NADPH, GST, total lipid, and GSH/GSSG (all p < 0.05; Tables S2 −S3). Consistent with these interactions, a decrease in NADPH levels within the coral host was observed in both the prometryn and its costress treatment groups (Figure 1D). Correlation analysis revealed that under combined stress, host NADPH levels were positively correlated with Symbiodiniaceae density and chlorophyll a

content (Figure S3), suggesting that damage to the symbiont and/or disruption of the symbiosis may reduce the supply of photosynthetic products (a potential source of NADPH precursors) to the host. Consequently, host assimilation metabolism and energy supply via the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) decline, leading to reduced NADPH gen-eration. 44 This NADPH depletion likely restricted the glutathione reductase (GR)-mediated reduction of GSSG back to GSH, 45 thereby exacerbating the decline in the GSH/GSSG ratio observed under prolonged exposure (Figure 2A and B). Concurrently, a short-term (2-day) increase in host GST activity indicated an initial activation of the oxidative stress response, which returned to baseline by day 9, possibly reflecting the eventual depletion of the glutathione pool due to sustained scavenging demand. 

3.2. Molecular Mechanisms Underlying Host and Symbiodiniaceae Responses to Combined Prometryn and Nitrate Enrichment Stress 

PCA revealed distinct group separations in both transcriptome and proteome profiles (Figures S4 −S6). In Symbiodiniaceae, prometryn exposure at environmental concentrations induced 389 DEGs after 2 days, increasing to 3,228 DEGs by day 9 (Figure S4). A similar accumulation pattern was observed in the coral host. Under combined prometryn −nitrate stress, DEGs in Symbiodiniaceae also increased progressively over time. These findings indicate that even at low environmental levels, prometryn significantly alters gene expression in both partners during prolonged exposure, likely due to its environmental persistence. 46 Transcriptomic and proteomic profiles further highlight the dominant role of prometryn under combined stress. 47 

Transcriptomic PCA analysis revealed that both the coral host and Symbiodiniaceae remained distinctly separated from the control group after 9 days of exposure to NP1 and NP5 costress treatments (Figures S5 and S6). Within the Calvin −Benson cycle, limited ATP/NADPH supply would constrain CO 2 assimilation and carbohydrate production. Consistently, downregulation of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase ( GAPDH ), triose-phosphate isomer-ase ( TPI ), and ribose-5-phosphate isomerase ( RPI ) (Figure S10A) indicates impaired NADPH-dependent reduction of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and per-turbed triose/pentose interconversions, thereby restricting carbon fixation. 50 These shifts likely reduce photosynthate availability and the energy derived from photosynthesis. Multiomics evidence indicates that coexposure drives Symbio-diniaceae to shift from energy-acquisition to release and storage pathways to meet elevated energy demand arising from reduced photosynthate. Similar energy-balance disruptions have been reported for cyanobacteria and corals exposed to pollutants. 42,51 

3.2.2. Costress from Prometryn and Nitrate May Disrupt Energy Metabolism and Induce Oxidative Stress in Coral Hosts. Under environmentally relevant coexposure, downregulated host-specific genes were enriched in plasma-membrane components and pathways including linoleic/ α-linolenic acid metabolism, protein digestion and absorption, and folate (tetrahydrofolate) metabolism (Figures S11 and S12). Consistently, downregulated proteins mapped to protein digestion and absorption (Figure S13). GSEA further showed progressive suppression after 9 days of glycolysis/gluconeo-genesis, the citrate (TCA) cycle, fatty acid degradation, and glutathione metabolism under coexposure (Figure 4A). Down-regulation of these genes and proteins perturbs oxidative stress defenses and energy metabolism (Figure 4). By day 6 of coexposure, significantly elevated ROS and MDA levels indicated the onset of a marked oxidative stress response in corals. 24 Oxidative stress is mitigated by antioxidant defenses, including SOD, CAT, GPx, GST, and GSH, which detoxify excess ROS and limit cellular damage. 52 When ROS production exceeds antioxidant capacity, membranes, proteins, and DNA incur oxidative damage. 52 In the host, downregulation of membrane-associated functions and glutathione metabolism may indicate oxidative injury and diminished antioxidant capacity; suppression of linoleic and α-linolenic acid metabolism further supports compromised membrane integrity. 53 Proteo-mic analysis revealed that the AMPK signaling pathway was significantly downregulated under prometryn −nitrate coexpo-sure (Figure S13B). Although AMPK is a classical activator of starvation-induced autophagy via mTOR inhibition, 54 its downregulation here, together with pronounced vacuolization and vacuolar matrices in Symbiodiniaceae, indicates that the observed autophagic processes are unlikely to be driven by energy shortage. Supporting an AMPK-independent route, a Ras-like GTP-binding protein involved in vesicle trafficking was upregulated, while a p62-like autophagy receptor (degraded during autophagic flux) was downregulated (Figure S13C and D). 55 −57 Taken together, these findings support a model in which costress activates a noncanonical, AMPK-independent autophagy −endolysosomal/endocytic axis, likely triggered by membrane damage and xenobiotic stress, consistent with the promotion of symbiophagy and vacuolar degradation of damaged symbionts. 58 

Coexposure also downregulated glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and the Calvin-Benson cycle, suggesting disruption of host energy metabolism, potentially compounded by reduced algal photosynthate supply. In cnidarian-algal symbiosis, nutrient exchange underpins mutualism, and excess algal photosynthate supports host energy demand. 59 Fatty acids are principal energy stores and biomarkers of coral nutritional status, and lipids have the greatest energy density among major biomolecules. 60 

Accordingly, inhibition of linoleic/ α-linolenic acid pathways implies impairedmobilization of stored energy under energy deficit. 

3.3. The Environmental Concentrations of Prometryn Increased the Susceptibility of Corals to Nitrate Stress 

Integrating physiological measurements with multiomics, we characterized Symbiodiniaceae and coral-host responses to environmentally relevant coexposure to prometryn and nitrate. Disentangling the relative contributions of each stressor remains a priority; our analyses implicate prometryn-induced photo-toxicity as the primary driver, whereas short-term nitrate enrichment elicits predominantly acclimatory responses in the host. Our findings underscore the urgency of mitigating the herbicide contamination in coastal waters. 

3.3.1. Environmental Concentrations of Prometryn May Increase the Energy Demands of Symbiodiniaceae in Response to Nitrate Enrichment. After day 2 of nitrate enrichment, Symbiodiniaceae showed substantially more down-regulated than upregulated genes (335 vs 78). By day 9, upregulated genes rose to 271, consistent with an early stress response followed by acclimation (Figure S4). In contrast, environmentally relevant prometryn produced substantially more differentially expressed genes at day 9 than at day 2, consistent with a delayed toxic response. 60 Multiomics integration showed concordant gene −protein changes enriched in energy metabolism, particularly photosynthesis and carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms (Figure S9A and D). Notably, nitrate enrichment alone upregulated proteins and genes in porphyrin/chlorophyll metabolism and photosynthesis, including uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase ( UROD ), LHC/ FCP-like antenna proteins, and petH (encoding ferredoxin −

The limited number of additional significant pathways under nitrate alone likely reflects that the nitrate level was near the lowest observed effect concentration (LOEC) (Figure 1A). Nevertheless, GSEA indicated overall upregulation of pathways linked to energy release and storage under nitrate enrichment, whereas these pathways were downregulated under prometryn alone (Figure 3B and Table S4). Consistent with this, nitrate enrichment alone increased expression of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACACA ) and 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase (PLK2 ) (Figure S10A); ACACA commits carbon to de novo fatty acid synthesis. 71 Similar trends were observed for TCA and oxidative phosphorylation components, including pyruvate dehydrogenase ( PDH ), iso-citrate dehydrogenase ( IDH ), and succinate dehydrogenase (SDH ) (Figure S10A). 3.3.2. Prometryn May Compromise Coral Host Antioxidant Defenses and Disrupt Energy Metabolism under Nitrate Enrichment. Under 2 days of exposure, a transient imbalance in the coral host’s nitrogen metabolism was observed under nitrate enrichment alone, as was reflected in inhibited glutamate dehydrogenase (NADP +) activity and fatty acid metabolism (including α-linolenic acid) (Figure S11A). Nitrogen assimilation via NADP-dependent glutamate dehy-drogenase (GDH) links ammonium incorporation to the TCA cycle by interconverting α-ketoglutarate and glutamate; reduced GDH activity, therefore, indicates diminished assimilatory capacity. 72 Furthermore, after 2 days of nitrate enrichment alone, we observed a transient but significant increase in GST levels, accompanied by a marked decrease in the GSH/GSSG ratio (Figure 2A and B). These findings indicate a state of acute oxidative stress under short-term exposure conditions. After 9 days of exposure, GSEA revealed significant enrichment of gene sets related to glutathione metabolism and protein digestion and absorption under nitrate-enrichment stress (Figure 4A). Notably, the expression of key genes involved in this pathway, including those encoding GST and glutathione hydrolase (GGT), was markedly upregulated (Figure 4B), highlighting their pivotal roles. At the same time, at the biochemical level, GST protein levels continued to increase, whereas the GSH/GSSG ratio returned to control levels (Figure 2A,B). Correlation analysis indicated that the induction of the GST gene closely paralleled the accumulation of its protein product (Figure 4C). Glutathione plays a pivotal role in regulating ROS levels, 69 and additionally, GST is indispensable for phase II drug metabolism, as it catalyzes the reduction of peroxide compounds that could otherwise exert cytotoxic effects. 73 These functional roles suggest that coral hosts may enhance their cytochrome P450 (CYP450) and GST activities to effectively detoxify harmful compounds and protect against oxidative stress. Consistently, previous studies have reported that corals can restore their antioxidant capacity by increasing the total antioxidant capacity during nitrate exposure. 74 

Host redox state did not recover in the costress group after 9 days (Figure 4B). Reduced photosynthate supply from Symbiodiniaceae, together with direct herbicide exposure, likely limited the compensatory use of lipid reserves. This is supported by the downregulation of multiple fatty acid metabolism proteins, including ACACA, ACADVL (acyl-CoA dehydrogen-ase), and CPT2 (carnitine O-palmitoyltransferase 2) (Figure 4D). CPT2 is a key component of the carnitine shuttle that transports long-chain fatty acyl groups into mitochondria for subsequent β-oxidation, 75 and ACADVL catalyzes the initial dehydrogenation step in mitochondrial β-oxidation of long-chain fatty acyl-CoAs. 76 Furthermore, acyl-CoA oxidase (ACOX) and PDH exhibited coordinated expression patterns under costress, and genes correlated with them were significantly enriched in the fatty acid elongation pathway, suggesting a linked disruption in both fatty acid catabolism and elongation (Figure S14). The strong correlation between RT-qPCR results and RNA-Seq data demonstrates the high reliability of the sequencing results (R 2 ≥ 0.80) (Figure S15). In the nitrate enrichment group, SDH and ubiquinol cytochrome c reductase core protein 2 (QCR2) were upregulated, indicating a compensatory enhancement of electron transport. In contrast, the costress group exhibited significant downregulation of multiple key mitochondrial OXPHOS proteins, including SDHA, QCR2, cytochrome c oxidase subunit 6C (COX6C), and ATP synthase subunit gamma (ATPeF1G) (Figure 4D). SDHA links the TCA cycle to OXPHOS and is pivotal for cellular ATP production. 77 −79 

ATPeF1G promotes ADP-Pi binding during ATP synthesis; its downregulation directly constrains ATP production. QCR2, a core subunit of complex III, transfers electrons from ubiquinol to cytochrome c, sustaining the proton-motive force that drives ATP generation. 78 Complexes I and III are the principal mitochondrial sites of superoxide formation. 80 Accordingly, QCR2 dysregulation impairs complex III assembly and enhances semiquinone leakage, thereby elevating mitochondrial ROS production. Thus, under costress, prooxidant production outpaces antioxidant capacity, leading to persistent oxidative stress. 81 The activation of protective pathways is energetically costly and may constrain aerobic scope. 82,83 When glucose-based pathways are constrained, corals may shift to lipid catabolism to meet energy demand. 84 We therefore propose that under nitrate enrichment alone, hosts adapt by increasing carbon-based photosynthate supply and enhancing fatty acid oxidation to support antioxidant defenses; however, prometryn diminishes this adaptive flexibility. This pattern aligns with nutrient −warming studies, in which nitrate exacerbates thermal bleaching while ammonium mitigates it through contrasting effects on lipid-mediated energy allocation and stress responses. 4 Collectively, these findings suggest that herbicide −

nitrate and nitrate −warming costresses share a common axis of vulnerability centered on lipid-supported energy buffering and antioxidant capacity. Consistent with an energy shortfall, the ribosome pathway was significantly downregulated in N9 vs NP1 (Figure S13A), and costress led to broad decreases in ribosomal proteins, including 60S RPL15-like, 60S RPL9-like, and a 40S S21-like protein (Figure 4D). Given that ribosome biogenesis and translation are energy-intensive, these results suggest that prometryn during nitrate enrichment limits cellular energy supply by inhibiting oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, thereby suppressing ribosome function and protein synthesis. Under prolonged exposure, this sustained energy deficit could lead to either acute symbiosis breakdown and rapid coral loss or chronic sublethal stress that progressively impairs reef growth and resilience. We suggest that under persistent coexposure, the observed metabolic suppression may translate into widespread sublethal stress, increasing reef vulnerability to other pressures and raising long-term ecological risk. Based on our findings, we propose a mechanism by which environmental concentrations of prometryn impair the ability of coral hosts and Symbiodiniaceae to cope with nitrate enrich-ment (Figure 5). Under costress, inhibition of photosynthetic pigment biosynthesis likely reduces carbon fixation and photosynthate supply from Symbiodiniaceae. Although Sym-biodiniaceae upregulate energy metabolism and antioxidant defenses in response to nitrate enrichment alone, prometryn disrupts these adaptive responses, increasing the energetic burden of nitrogen stress and limiting the host’s ability to mobilize stored fatty acids. At higher prometryn concentrations (approaching the EC 50 ), the imbalance between energy supply and demand is amplified, leading to more severe oxidative damage.

3.4. Environmental Implications 

Herbicide leakage into seawater has been recognized as a pervasive threat to marine primary productivity and zooplank-ton communities globally. 20 In coastal ecosystems, herbicide and nitrate pollution frequently co-occur, and their potential synergistic effects on coral reefs warrant careful investigation. Our study demonstrates that environmentally relevant concen-trations of the herbicide prometryn may impair the acclimation capacity of corals to nitrate enrichment. Specifically, prometryn-induced phototoxicity in Symbiodiniaceae appears to disrupt the host’s glutathione-based antioxidant response to nitrate and reduce photosynthate transfer, thereby compromising energy homeostasis and exacerbating oxidative stress. Although the phototoxic effect of prometryn on Symbiodiniaceae has been established, its precise molecular targets remain unclear. Computational modeling should be further applied to elucidate the binding affinity of prometryn for photosynthetic antenna proteins. Furthermore, additional work is needed to quantify shifts in energy partitioning and lipid composition between coral hosts and their symbionts under combined stress. Moving forward, a multiscale research framework is essential. This should range from elucidating molecular mechanisms of PSII damage and repair to assessing intraspecific genetic variation in tolerance and longer-term responses beyond the relatively short exposure used here and ultimately to evaluating community-level impacts on reef biodiversity. 85 −87 In particular, screening for herbicide-tolerant coral genotypes could inform selective breeding strategies for reef restoration in polluted areas. Overall, this work contributes to the growing body of evidence indicating that coral bleaching risk assessments must explicitly account for the co-occurrence of local nutrient enrichment and photosynthesis-inhibiting herbicide contamination. Addition-ally, our findings underscore the necessity of integrated land-sea management to address nonpoint source pollution. In light of the demonstrated synergism between nitrate and prometryn, future research and management of seawater quality stressors should consider their combined impacts on corals rather than regulating each pollutant individually.
